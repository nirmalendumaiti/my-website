PhD 2026

Representation of Postcolonial Eco-geographical Spaces in Select Works of Amitav Ghosh, Pankaj Sekhsaria, Kiran Desai and Stephen Alter

My PhD thesis explores how European colonialism and the modern urban ‘developments’ promote anthropocentric binaries between nature and culture, and how eco-cultural balance between the wildlife, the environment, and the local communities get disrupted because of that. My PhD dissertation studies four distinct Indian eco-geographical spaces represented in four selected novels: the Sundarbans delta in The Hungry Tide, the islands of Andaman and Nicobar in The Last Wave, the mountain spaces of Darjeeling and Kalimpong in The Inheritance of Loss, and the terai space of the Sarda Valley region in Uttarakhand in In the Jungles of the Night.

Postcolonial Ecocriticism · Environmental Humanities · Anthropocentrism
Research Paper 2025

Living Garbage and the ‘Wasteland’ of Edenic Andaman: ‘Dumped’ Communities and Disrupted Island Eco-space in Pankaj Sekhsaria’s The Last Wave

My paper interrogates what it means to live in the ‘Wasteocene.’ The paper expands the concepts of “waste” and “garbage”. Here I have examined how colonialism and colonialism-induced 'developments' transform the Andaman Islands into dumping grounds for unwanted human and nonhuman lives, generating spatial violence and ecological conflicts among mainland refugees, Indigenous community of the Jarawas, and the fragile island ecosystems in Pankaj Sekhsaria’s The Last Wave.

Wasteocene · Living Garbage · Island Space
Book Chapter 2020

Shadow Lines: The Border, the “Other”, and the Eco-literary in The Hungry Tide and The Great Derangement

In this paper we examine how Amitav Ghosh’s The Shadow Lines, The Hungry Tide, and The Great Derangement expose the parallels between geopolitical borders and the human/nonhuman conflicts. Taking Ghosh’s concept of the borderline from The Shadow Lines, this article explores the author’s employment of the notion of border between the human and the non-human, the human exploitation and the violence exerted on the non-human and the environmental anxiety which finds eco-literary expression in his fiction The Hungry Tide (2004) and the non-fiction The Great Derangement (2016). We have explored here how the “‘partitioning” or deepening the imaginary gulf between Nature and Culture” (GD 92) has contributed to the age-old indifference towards eco-literary expressions as simple nature writings and how climate literature is not just a literary “other” but an urgent demand of time with references to The Great Derangement.

Shadow Lines · Border-Boundaries
